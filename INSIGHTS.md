# Gameplay Insights

These three observations come from exploring the February 10–14, 2026 production telemetry (89,104 records across 796 matches) using the Lila Black visualization tool. They highlight how recorded players actually moved, where they fought, and how different spaces functioned during the playtest sessions.

---

## Insight 1 — Busy Routes Aren't Always Combat Hotspots

### What caught my eye

When toggling between the **Traffic** heatmap and the **Kills** and **Deaths** heatmaps on AmbroseValley, I noticed that several heavily traveled pathways have almost no recorded fights. Players moved through them frequently, but the combat markers clustered elsewhere in tighter crossroads and bottlenecks. High player traffic did not automatically mean high combat activity.

### Evidence

- **Map traffic:** On AmbroseValley, there are 48,754 recorded movement samples (`Position` + `BotPosition`).
- **Traffic concentration:** The top 10% of traffic cells (205 cells on a 64×64 grid) account for 21,962 of those samples (**45.05%** of all movement).
- **Zero-combat transit corridors:** Out of those top 205 busy cells, **55 cells recorded zero combat events** (`Kill`, `BotKill`, `Killed`, or `BotKilled`).
- **Corridor volume:** Those 55 peaceful cells carry **5,215 movement samples (10.70% of the entire map’s recorded traffic)**. For example, two adjacent western cells around `UV [0.117, 0.586]` logged 314 and 231 movement samples respectively, with zero recorded combat.
- **Combat clustering:** By comparison, combat is much more localized. The top 10% of combat cells hold **866 of the 2,287 recorded combat events (37.87%)**, clustering heavily around the central intersection where the combat-event-to-traffic-sample ratio exceeds 12% to 13%.

### What this could affect

- Combat concentration across the map
- The ratio of combat events to traffic samples along major arteries
- Route usage and player willingness to rotate
- Share of traffic passing through combat-active cells

### Action

Possible level-design tests:

1. **Investigate low-combat transit corridors:** Review whether long, quiet routes are intentional rotation highways or if players are bypassing key encounter zones too easily. Test adjusting sightlines or adding minor objective callouts, and monitor whether **route usage** and **player exposure to combat** change.
2. **Review central combat bottlenecks:** Test adding alternate flanking routes or tiered cover around the central crossroads, and track whether **combat concentration** in the top 10% of cells drops and whether **traffic/combat ratios** distribute more evenly.

### Why a level designer should care

A busy path is not automatically a combat space. Knowing which routes players use strictly for travel versus where they actually fight helps a level designer decide the space's purpose: should it remain a clean, low-friction rotation corridor, or does it need geometry changes to support deliberate fireteams and tactical skirmishes?

## Insight 2 — Recorded Combat Activity Is Dominated by Human-vs-Bot Events

### What caught my eye

Looking at the event toggles and entity breakdowns in the sidebar, recorded combat activity was overwhelmingly dominated by human players killing AI bots, while explicitly recorded PvP kills were extremely sparse.

### Evidence

- **Recorded kills:** Across the deduplicated telemetry used by the visualization, there are **2,379 recorded kill events** (`Kill` + `BotKill`).
- **2,376 `BotKill` events** were recorded: **2,193 (92.30%)** were generated by human-classified entities, while **183 (7.70%)** were generated by bot-classified entities.
- There were only **3 `Kill` events (0.13%)**. Because the telemetry does not record victim identity, these cannot be reliably classified as human-versus-human kills.
- **Loot pickups:** Across the deduplicated telemetry, there are **11,632 `Loot` events**.
  - **11,528 (99.11%)** were generated by human-classified entities.
  - **104 (0.89%)** were generated by bot-classified entities.   
- **Client context:** In 795 of the 796 recorded matches, telemetry was logged from the perspective of a single recorded human client encountering proximate bots.


### What this could affect

- Bot encounter frequency and engagement pacing
- Bot kill rate and player survival rate
- Loot acquisition rate across early and late match phases
- Bot combat effectiveness and threat level
- Overall PvE engagement frequency prior to extraction

### Action

Investigate whether the bot encounters provide the intended tension and pacing:

1. **Test bot density and patrol placement:** Test spacing out bot spawns along high-traffic perimeter POIs versus central objectives. Monitor the **bot encounter frequency** and **bot kill rate** per session.
2. **Review loot rewards around bot outposts:** Test linking higher-tier loot containers to aggressive bot camps, and monitor **loot acquisition rates** and whether players alter their routes to seek out or avoid these encounters.

### Why a level designer should care

If over 92% of recorded kill events are human-generated `BotKill` events and 99.11% of loot events are generated by human-classified players, bot placement and encounter-space geometry are important parts of the recorded combat and resource loop. Placing bots in narrow chokepoints can create stronger traversal pressure, while placing them near open loot nodes can create more optional encounters.

## Insight 3 — AmbroseValley Has More Concentrated Recorded Movement

### What caught my eye

Comparing the Traffic heatmaps across all three maps in the tool, AmbroseValley showed a sharper contrast between high-traffic arteries and areas with little or no recorded movement than Lockdown or GrandRift. A relatively small share of the map accounted for a large share of the recorded movement.

### Evidence

- **Movement concentration:** On AmbroseValley (48,754 position samples):
  - The top 5% of active traffic cells (103 cells on a 64×64 grid) holds **15,106 samples (30.98% of all recorded movement)**.
  - The top 10% of active cells (205 cells) holds **21,962 samples (45.05%)**.
- **Cross-map comparison:**
  - On AmbroseValley, the top 5% of active cells carries **30.98%** of recorded movement.
  - On Lockdown, the top 5% of active cells carries **23.68%** (4,399 of 18,577 samples).
  - On GrandRift, the top 5% of active cells carries **23.52%** (1,347 of 5,728 samples).
- **Cells with no recorded movement:** More than half of the AmbroseValley 64×64 grid contained no recorded movement telemetry in this dataset (2,045 active cells out of 4,096 total cells, meaning 2,051 cells had zero recorded traffic samples).

### What this could affect

- Overall traffic concentration across map quadrants
- Route diversity and predictability of rotations
- Percentage of movement concentrated in the top 5% and 10% of cells
- Utilization of lower-traffic peripheral regions
- Use of lower-traffic regions during rotations

### Action

Evaluate experiments to spread player rotations more naturally:

1. **Test dynamic or peripheral extraction points:** Experiment with placing extraction beacons or high-value contracts in lower-traffic quadrants. Monitor whether the **percentage of movement in the top 5% of cells** decreases and whether **route diversity** increases.
2. **Test alternate transition corridors:** Introduce secondary traversable routes or clear landmarks connecting quiet corners to the center. Monitor whether **traffic share in lower-traffic regions** rises during mid-match rotations.

### Why a level designer should care

When movement is concentrated into a small number of cells, those routes become important parts of the map's overall traffic flow. Identifying quieter sectors gives a designer a starting point for testing whether secondary objectives, traversal options, or extraction choices can diversify movement without disrupting the main routes.

## Methodology

- **Coordinate transform:** Raw world `(x, z)` coordinates were mapped to normalized `[0, 1]` map coordinates using each map's documented origin and scale bounding box.
- **Grid aggregation:** Movement and combat events were grouped into uniform 64×64 spatial grid bins (4,096 cells) to compare spatial density consistently across maps.
- **Event definitions:**
  - *Traffic:* `Position` + `BotPosition`
  - *Combat:* `Kill` + `BotKill` + `Killed` + `BotKilled`
  - *Loot:* `Loot`
- **Entity identification:** Numeric user IDs denote server bots; UUID strings denote human client players.

---

## Important limitations

- **Single-client perspective:** 99.9% of matches in this dataset record telemetry from one human client plus nearby bots, rather than a full-lobby server perspective.
- **No proof of inaccessibility:** Areas without recorded movement show where recorded players didn't travel during these sessions, not that those spaces are physically inaccessible or unused by other players.
- **Timestamp resolution:** Timestamps have one-second precision, so intra-second updates share the same second value.
- **Combat telemetry details:** `Kill` and `BotKill` events record attacker position but do not record the victim's identity or location.
- **Sparse storm data:** Only 39 storm deaths occurred across all five days, which is too sparse to support reliable density insights.
